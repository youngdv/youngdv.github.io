Helping Ports Talk to Each Other

I designed a trusted dashboard that connects port operators, truck dispatchers, and cargo owners — replacing a system of endless phone calls and data-hoarding with a single, secure source of truth.

The Challenge

The Privacy Gap

Ports are complex. Every shipment touches terminal operators, truck dispatchers, and cargo owners — but nobody wants to share their data for fear of losing a competitive edge. This causes massive cargo delays, wasted trips, and a system held together by phone calls.

My job was to design a system that feels safe enough for competitors to share data, while staying simple enough for stressed operators to actually use. I used Behavioral Science to solve a trust problem, not just a UI problem.

Behavioral Science

The Formula

Every design decision mapped to a user pain point and a behavioral principle.

The User Pain Point My Design Solution The Science Behind It
"I don't trust the system." Cargo owners fear sharing data will reveal trade secrets to competitors. Privacy-First Data Masking: A view that shows a cargo's status (Ready/Delayed) without revealing its contents or value. Psychological Safety: People won't use a tool if they feel exposed. I built trust into the UI by giving users granular control over what they share.
"I'm overwhelmed." Terminal Operators manage thousands of containers and can't find the "fire" in a mountain of data. Exception-Based Alerts: Instead of showing everything, the UI only surfaces when a delay occurs. Cognitive Load: By filtering out "normal" data, I reduced the mental tax on operators so they can focus only on what needs fixing.
"I'm guessing." Truck dispatchers send drivers to pick up cargo that isn't actually ready yet — wasting fuel and time. Real-Time Sync: A live dashboard that updates the moment a container is cleared by the terminal. Reducing Uncertainty: High stress comes from a lack of control. Giving dispatchers real-time data lowers anxiety and prevents dry runs.
Who I Helped

Three People. Three Different Needs.

Carlos — Terminal Operator

Goal: Keep the port moving and containers flowing out the gate.

Problem: Alert fatigue — too many notifications, no way to spot the real emergencies.

My fix: Simplified his dashboard to surface only exceptions. Green means go, bold red means act now.

David — Cargo Owner (BCO)

Goal: Track his goods and know when they're ready for pickup.

Problem: Fear of data leaks — he doesn't want competitors seeing his shipment details.

My fix: Role-based views with privacy masking. He controls exactly what's visible to others.

Maria — Dispatcher

Goal: Coordinate trucks efficiently without sending drivers on wasted trips.

Problem: Making 20+ phone calls a day just to confirm container status.

My fix: A single source of truth — live container status that updates the moment things change.

Research

Designing Without a Research Budget

This was a fast-moving project with limited field time. I leaned on my Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) background to de-risk the design without slowing down delivery.

  • Heuristic Evaluation: I audited every screen against standard mental models for logistics professionals to catch usability issues before they reached users.
  • Task Analysis: I mapped the full "life of a shipment" to find the exact moments where communication breaks down between stakeholders.
  • Role-Based Logic: I worked directly with the Solutions Architect to ensure the UI matched the strict security and data-access requirements of the port ecosystem.
Design

Making It Better — The Behavioral Upgrades

Three specific design decisions that came directly from behavioral principles:

  • Progressive Disclosure: Complex shipping details live behind a "Details" button. The main screen stays clean so operators aren't overwhelmed on load.
  • Visual Hierarchy: "Delayed" status indicators are bold and high-contrast — the first thing an operator's eye lands on, even when scanning fast.
  • Common Language: I replaced technical port jargon with plain language that both truck drivers and port admins understand. No more translation tax.
Safety Design

The Hazardous Cargo Workflow

Hazmat is the highest-stakes workflow in the port ecosystem. Different regulations, different handling requirements, different approval chains. A mistake here has real safety consequences.

  • Visual differentiation: Hazmat containers flagged with persistent warning indicators — not just color, but iconography and placement changes that make them impossible to miss
  • Mandatory confirmation steps: Approval workflows require explicit acknowledgment of cargo class before any status change
  • Cross-stakeholder visibility: When hazmat status changes, all relevant stakeholders get proactive notifications — not just the next person in the chain
  • Audit trail: Every action logged with timestamps and actor ID for compliance
Validation

Evidence Over Opinions

I turned a standard User Acceptance Test into a usability study — creating specific tasks for 9+ port stakeholders to complete. The goal: quantify where the mental load was still too high.

What We Tested What Users Did The Design Fix
Finding cargo status
Can a Dispatcher find a specific container in under 10 seconds?		 90% of users found it immediately thanks to high-contrast status badges. Validated — the visual hierarchy I built held up under pressure.
Data privacy
Can a BCO successfully mask their private trade data?		 Friction point: users weren't sure their privacy settings had saved. Added a "Privacy Confirmed" badge so users feel psychologically safe before sharing.
Error handling
What happens when an operator enters the wrong container ID?		 Users missed the small error text at the bottom of the screen. Moved error alerts to the center of the focus zone to prevent inattentional blindness.

The Final Scorecard

85% Task success rate on first try, without help
50% faster Time to find a shipment delay vs. the old phone-call method
4 out of 5 Users felt "more confident" in data accuracy than the previous system
The Outcome

From Theory to Tool

The PCIS Dashboard moved from a complex data-sharing concept to a functional, 508-compliant platform. By focusing on Trust and Cognitive Load, I created a system that doesn't just display data — it actually coordinates the people who keep our supply chain moving.

  • Three distinct role-based dashboards — terminal operators, dispatchers, and cargo owners each see exactly what they need, nothing they don't
  • Usability testing with 9+ port stakeholders hit an 85% first-try task success rate with no assistance — the behavioral design held up under real-world pressure
  • Achieved stakeholder alignment on shared terminology and workflow definitions — a prerequisite for the technical build that had no prior solution
  • Proof of concept validated the design direction and set the blueprint for all future development phases
